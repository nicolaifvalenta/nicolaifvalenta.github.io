There is a particular silence that follows a good objection. Everyone in the room has noticed it, the speaker most of all, and for a second nobody knows whether the next move is a reply or a retreat.[^1]

I used to think that the right response was always a reply. Lately I think the better habit is to say, out loud, "I don't know yet." It sounds like a small thing. It is surprisingly hard.

## Why it is hard

Part of it is professional. We are trained to defend. But part of it is something I would call a mistake about what a position is. A position is not a possession; it is closer to a bet that you are willing to revisit when the odds move.[^2] I wrote about the formal side of this in [a short note on conditionals and belief](/writing/a-note-on-modal-logic/), if you care to look.

If you hover over that link, you should see the opening of the note.

<hr>

The silence is the interesting part. I want to say more about it another time.

[^1]: Sometimes the silence is the speaker deciding whether the objection is actually good. This takes longer than it looks.
[^2]: This is not quite Bayesian, though it is friendly to it. I mean only that holding a view and holding it *dogmatically* are different things, and the second is the one worth avoiding.
